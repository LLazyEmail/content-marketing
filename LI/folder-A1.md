
**1. The road into Porkrovsk**

For a while, there was really only one road into Porkrovsk that mattered most.

Everything moved along it: supplies, people, casualties. Russian drones watched the route, especially the choke point beneath the bridge. 
In five to seven days, our battalion lost eight or nine vehicles there.

And still, every day, two of our cars drove back and forth along that same stretch to reach our team.

I remember watching the wrecks accumulate on the roadside. I knew the drones were watching. I knew what could happen on the next run. 
And then someone got in the car and went anyway, because the people at the other end needed them.

Before the front shifted, this was just a road we used for ordinary errands—picking up tools and building supplies. Today, that area is occupied. The ordinary version of it feels very far away.

What stays with me most isn’t a dramatic moment. It’s the drivers doing the same dangerous trip again the next day, and the day after that.

**2. The river ridge**

During certification, I found one small way to clear my head: rent a bike and ride toward the river.

The trail led up to a ridge, shaded by trees, with a wide view over the water. 
It was quiet there—quiet enough to forget, for a little while, how heavy everything else felt.

The land has a long memory. 
Those bluffs had once been useful to Cossacks watching the river: high ground, cover, and a clear view of the fastest route toward the city.
The same features that made the ridge strategically important also make it a beautiful place to stop and catch your breath.

I’d ride out, stand at the overlook, and watch the river move below.
It’s a strange feeling, being somewhere that has seen so much conflict and finding it peaceful again.

For a little while, the ridge was just a place to breathe.

**3. The electric bikes**

We were looking for any way to move supplies without drawing attention. Electric bikes seemed like a good idea: quiet, quick, and easier to use than a vehicle.

On paper, anyway.

A bike rated for 20 km/h feels different when you’re wearing armor, carrying a heavy pack, and trying to keep extra equipment in one hand. It’s not exactly a relaxing ride.

There’s a photo from that time where my teammate looks deeply unimpressed with the camera. He muttered, “Don’t take that picture. What if it’s my last one?” That kind of dark humor was common at the front. Sometimes it was the only sensible response.

The next day, I learned another lesson. I was riding with water in my pack and equipment in my left hand when I heard a sharp sound behind me. In a war full of FPV drones, you don’t ignore a sound like that. I turned to check the sky, lost control, and went face-first into the dirt.

The bikes were useful for sometime. Then the conditions caught up with them, and we switched to more reliable options.

Not every good idea survives contact with the field. Sometimes the lesson comes with a mouthful of dirt.

**4. The house**

It’s a remarkable thing that all four of us made it out of that house alive.

For a few days, it almost felt like a home. We slept upstairs and used whatever dry goods and spices the owners had left behind. We worked and cooked in the shelter below. It was cold, but having separate places to sleep and work felt like a luxury.

Then three KAB strikes landed nearby.

A shell tore through the window and blew out part of the wall. My first thought was the guy cooking in the kitchen. I ran to check on him. He was alive and unhurt—just badly shaken by how close it had been.

After that, we moved underground for good. Sleeping in a  dirt shelter suddenly seemed like a fair trade for being below ground.

When I look at the photos now, I don’t just see a hole in a wall. I remember the few seconds when we didn’t know if everyone was okay—and the relief when we found out.

**5. The patch**

A patch on a technician’s sleeve can carry more meaning than you’d expect.

When care packages arrived from across the ocean(ok ok ok - from a Germany), they weren’t just supplies. They were a reminder that people far away were thinking about us—and practical help at a time when it mattered. Good cold-weather gear, tourniquets, and solid clothing made a real difference.

The gear didn’t sit around for long. It went straight to the guys in position, where it was needed.

I put the patch on our lead technician’s sleeve. That felt right. The technicians were the people keeping our vehicles running under difficult conditions, often with very little room to work and plenty of reasons not to be out in the open.

For him, the patch was a small mark of pride. For me, it was a reminder of the people who helped get useful things into the hands of the people who needed them.

**6. Crimea**

I met Ilya aka Crimea — after he chose to leave a safe assignment and come to the front.

We’d both done infantry training in Brittany. The first thing I noticed about him wasn’t his attitude, though. It was his backpack. Mine had the permanent look of mud, dust, and hard use. His British-issue pack looked brand new.

I remember thinking, “What the hell, dude?” It was funny, especially because the contrast said so much without either of us needing to explain it.

He had done his time guarding a safer post. But he didn’t want to sit it out. He wanted to be where the work was.

That kind of motivation is hard to miss. The clean backpack didn’t stay clean for long, but his drive was there from the start.

**7. Yuri**

I keep coming back to this photo of Yuri in Luhansk in 2014.

He’s standing in front of an angry crowd, holding the Ukrainian flag. He looks steady in the picture, but I know that steadiness was a choice. He understood early that the armories mattered. He worked with local police to help secure them, knowing what could happen if the weapons fell into the hands of Russian-backed forces.

He stood his ground when not everyone understood how serious the threat was. And he stayed in the fight.

Yuri was killed in Mariupol during the first, devastating months of the full-scale invasion.

When I look at this old photo now, I don’t just see someone holding a flag in Luhansk. I see a friend who spent years trying to protect his home and the people in it.

I wish he were still here.

**8. The Four Kings**

“Four Kings” wasn’t a title we came up with for a photo. It was earned over three years.

Building a unit from scratch means sharing the cold, the sleepless shifts, the constant pressure, and all the unglamorous work that keeps things going. You learn who shows up. You learn who doesn’t need to be asked twice.

One of the guys in this photo came from infantry reconnaissance. I watched him grow into the role, and he never hesitated when the work needed doing. He’s in a safer, better place now, and I’m glad of it.

People come and go. Those four stayed at the core.

We kept showing up through one phase of the war after another. Then we took on more, built new capabilities, and made the unit stronger for the people who joined later.

The photo shows three of us. The history behind it includes all four.

**9. Yogurt in Germany**

I look at this photo and remember the yogurt.

It was a free Sunday at a training ground in Germany. I was sitting outside, eating from a container with a wooden spoon I’d bought at the Lviv border crossing. For a few minutes, there was nothing to do but eat and enjoy the sun.

That spoon made me smile. It was a small piece of home in a place where we were learning new systems and preparing for whatever came next.

At the time, the future was still open. We were learning about the German armor and its capabilities. We didn’t yet know how much the battlefield would change.

Later, the sky filled with FPV drones, and the loud, heavy vehicles became much harder to operate without being spotted. The war changed quickly, and the equipment available never quite matched the scale of the need.

But the photo still holds onto that Sunday: the sun, the wooden spoon, and a quiet moment to eat and breathe before everything got louder.

**10. A field test**

Field testing new equipment is one thing. Field testing something with a 50-meter blast radius is another.

We had a new round, a printed instruction sheet, and a lot of responsibility. Every connection and every step mattered. A mistake wouldn’t just mean a failed test; it could put our own people at risk.

Our unit had been asked to test the setup. We worked through it in freezing conditions, following the instructions and figuring out how to make the system work safely.

What I remember most isn’t a dramatic victory. It’s the focus: cold hands, paper instructions, and the knowledge that there was no room to be careless.

The people around me brought a lot of technical skill to a difficult problem. That mattered more than any heroic-sounding summary could.

**11. Making the replacement part**

When important equipment kept breaking and the factory had no spare parts, we had two choices: accept the delay or try to solve it ourselves.

Yuri and I sat down with the broken components and worked out their shape. We built a 3D model, then sent it to two metal shops to see whether they could make replacements.

They could.

The best part was that we didn’t keep the solution to ourselves. We shared the design so other units facing the same problem could use it too.

It was a simple process: understand what failed, make a replacement, and pass the solution on.

A broken part had become a supply problem. A shared design turned it into something other people could fix too.
